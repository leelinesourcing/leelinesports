**B2B Blog Humour Writing Guide**  
*(Optimised for professional audiences who still have a pulse)*

### The North Star
Your reader is a 28–55-year-old procurement manager, operations director, or founder who has sat through too many death-by-PowerPoint webinars. They’re busy, sceptical, and secretly relieved when someone in the industry sounds like a human instead of a LinkedIn robot.

**Voice:** Dry-witted industry veteran who’s seen every mistake twice and still shows up with solutions. Think “seasoned consultant who’s been in the game 20+ years” meets “guy in the airport lounge who buys the first round.”  
Self-deprecating, never condescending. Punch up at yourself, the industry, or the absurdities of business — never at the reader.

### The Opening Rule
If the first 50 words don’t contain at least one wry observation, industry pun, or self-aware aside, rewrite them. You are not a brochure. You are the person they actually want to grab coffee with.

**Good energy examples:**
- “Our ERP migration went so smoothly the project manager started speaking to us again. This is not normal.”
- “Most supply-chain software promises the world. Ours just promises not to ghost you at 3 a.m. when the warehouse goes down.”
- “Cashflow forecasting is a lot like marriage — everyone says they’re committed until the first unexpected expense shows up.”

### Five Rules for B2B Humour (Every Post)

**1. Industry puns are mandatory — but tasteful.**  
Deliver the pun, then immediately deliver value. No winking.  
- “We specialise in processes that don’t bottleneck harder than Monday morning traffic.”  
- “Our platform has more uptime than most New Year’s resolutions.”  
- “Legacy systems: the corporate equivalent of keeping your 2009 BlackBerry ‘just in case’.”

**2. Punch at yourself and the industry, never the reader.**  
- “After 22 years implementing this stuff, I still sometimes call the integration layer ‘the magic bit’ in internal meetings.”  
- “My team keeps a jar for every time I say ‘seamless’. We’re funding someone’s Hawaii trip by Q3.”  
- “The consultant in me wants to say it’s complicated. The honest version is: most vendors overpromise because it’s easier than under-delivering well.”

**3. Parenthetical asides = personality injection.**  
Use them like stage directions:  
- (Yes, I’m aware how corporate that sounds. No, I won’t stop.)  
- (The fact you’re still reading suggests either genuine interest or you’re avoiding another status meeting.)  
- [Pauses for dramatic effect while the integration finishes syncing.]

**4. Safe pop-culture references that won’t age out.**  
Seinfeld, The Office, Shawshank, Die Hard, Jaws, classic Simpsons, dad-rock.  
- “This is the ‘we need a bigger boat’ moment of vendor selection.”  
- “If The Office taught us anything, it’s that most process improvements die in committee.”  
- “Trying to fix broken reporting with spreadsheets is basically the Shawshank of data management — possible, but you’ll lose years of your life.”

**5. The sign-off must land.**  
End with a smile, not a sales brochure line.  
- “If your current system is costing you sleep (or margin), give us a call. We’ll fix it — and probably tell you one more terrible pun for free.”  
- “Worst case, you get a decent conversation. Best case, you stop paying for software that treats you like it’s still 2012.”  

### Anti-Patterns (Never Do These)

- Exclamation marks. Humour works better deadpan.  
- Emojis. This isn’t Slack.  
- Punching down at customers or prospects. Ever.  
- Current TikTok/Gen-Z slang. Your audience is making payroll, not “rizzing up” deals.  
- Trying to be edgy or “disruptive.” You’re not a VC Twitter bro.  
- Repeating the same pun in one post. One flapper joke is enough.  
- Meta jokes about “writing this blog post.” Once per quarter, maximum.  
- Corporate zombie language (“synergising deliverables”) unless you’re immediately roasting it.

### Frequency & Flow
In a 1,200–1,800 word post aim for:  
- 3–5 industry puns or observations  
- 2–3 self-deprecating moments  
- 1–2 parenthetical asides  
- One clean, memorable closer  

Read it aloud in your head. Does it sound like a competent, slightly cheeky peer who respects your time? Good.  
Does it sound like marketing trying to be funny? Back to the drawing board.

### Final Vibe Check
Before publishing, ask:  
Would a cynical procurement director forward this to their team with “worth a read”?  
If yes → ship.  
If it feels like a brochure with jokes stapled on → optimise again.

This isn’t about being the funniest person in B2B.  
It’s about being the only one who doesn’t sound like they were written by committee.

Now go make enterprise software, compliance, logistics, or [your industry] content that people actually enjoy reading.
